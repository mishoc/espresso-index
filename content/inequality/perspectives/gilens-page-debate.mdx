## The disagreement

Whose preferences actually shape U.S. federal policy — the median voter, economic elites, organized interest groups? The answer bears directly on inequality: if policy tracks the rich, rising inequality will not trigger the redistributive correction that majoritarian models expect.

## Viewpoint A — elites and organized interests dominate

<Cite id="gilens-page-2014" /> tested four theories of American policymaking against 1,779 policy issues from 1981–2002 and found that economic elites and organized business interests have substantial independent influence on outcomes, while average citizens' preferences have close to zero independent effect once elite preferences are controlled for. Read alongside <Cite id="meltzer-richard-1981" />, whose median-voter model predicts democracies should demand more redistribution as inequality rises, the finding offers a mechanism for why that prediction failed in the United States.

**Strongest evidence:** a large, systematic dataset across two decades, with a clear multivariate design.

## Viewpoint B — the "zero effect" claim is overstated

The library entry records the critiques: scholars such as Omar Bashir (2015) argue that how "average citizen preferences" were measured, and the small effective sample of issues on which elites and the public actually disagreed, make the near-zero estimate fragile — on most issues the two groups want the same thing, so the data cannot cleanly separate their influence. The dataset also ends in 2002 and has not been replicated with more recent policy data.

**Strongest evidence:** the statistical critique goes to the design, not just the interpretation, and the authors' own data show elite and mass preferences usually align.

## Where the field stands

The paper is best read together with its critics, as an influential and contested result rather than settled fact. A replication with post-2002 data and better-specified models of preference intensity would meaningfully update it and has not yet been done.
